Reverse flip to India: bringing your holding company home

Startups that flipped to Delaware for investors are now flipping back — usually to list in India. The rules have become friendlier since 2024, but the tax bill on the way out can be substantial. Here’s the map.

By CA Durgesh Chavda·Last reviewed 26 September 2026·9 min read
Plan a reverse flip hello@durgeshchavda.com

Why companies flip back

  • Listing in India: an Indian IPO generally needs an Indian listed entity at the top.
  • Investor base: more capital is now available from Indian funds and public markets.
  • Simplicity: one main jurisdiction instead of a US parent with an Indian operating company, two sets of compliance and transfer pricing.
  • Regulatory access: some sectors and government programmes favour Indian-owned-and-controlled companies.

Several well-known startups have completed reverse flips in recent years. For the original decision, see the Delaware flip guide.

What changed in 2024–2026

  • Share swaps (August 2024): amendments to the FEMA non-debt instrument rules clarified that non-residents can acquire Indian company shares in exchange for shares of a foreign company, aligning with the overseas investment rules.
  • Fast-track merger (September 2024): the Companies (Compromises, Arrangements and Amalgamations) Rules were amended so that a foreign holding company merging into its wholly owned Indian subsidiary can use the fast-track route under Section 233 — approved by the Regional Director rather than the NCLT — with prior RBI approval.
  • Wider fast-track (September 2025): further amendments extended the fast-track route to more categories of mergers.
  • RBI cross-border merger amendments (May 2026): the RBI amended its Cross-Border Merger Regulations to better align FEMA with the company-law process.

Indian tax

  • Indian tax law provides exemptions for certain amalgamations and for shareholders receiving shares in an amalgamation, where the prescribed conditions are met — which is one reason the merger route is popular.
  • Share swaps need a careful look at capital-gains treatment for Indian-resident shareholders of the US company and at valuation.
  • From 1 April 2026 the analysis runs under the Income-tax Act, 2025 — check the corresponding provisions for transactions after that date.

US tax: the big variable

Moving the top company out of the US is an outbound transaction for US tax, and it is often where the largest costs arise:

  • Shareholders and the US company can be taxed on built-in gains when US shares or assets move to a foreign company, depending on how the transaction is structured.
  • Anti-inversion rules may apply when former US-company shareholders end up owning most of the new Indian parent. At the highest continuity levels, the Indian company can be treated as a US corporation for US tax — unless an exception applies, most commonly where the group has substantial business activities in India, tested against employee, asset and income thresholds.
  • IP held in the US — moving it abroad can trigger its own US tax charge.

Companies that reverse-flipped ahead of IPOs have reportedly paid significant tax to do so. US tax advice is essential before choosing a route.

Other workstreams

  • ESOPs: US-parent options must be replaced or converted into Indian-company options — see US parent ESOPs.
  • Investor rights in the Delaware documents move into the Indian articles and shareholders’ agreement — within what Indian law and listing rules allow.
  • FEMA reporting for the new foreign shareholders of the Indian company — see FC-GPR and FC-TRS.
  • Winding down the US entity if it isn’t needed — see closing a Delaware company.

Frequently asked questions

What is a reverse flip?
Moving a startup's holding company from abroad — commonly Delaware — back to India, usually through an inbound merger of the foreign parent into its Indian subsidiary or a share swap, so the Indian company becomes the top entity.
What is the fast-track route for reverse flips?
Since September 2024, a foreign holding company merging into its wholly owned Indian subsidiary can use the fast-track merger process under Section 233 of the Companies Act, approved by the Regional Director instead of the NCLT, with prior RBI approval.
Is a reverse flip taxable in the US?
It can be. Moving the parent company out of the US is an outbound transaction; shareholders and the US company may be taxed on built-in gains, and anti-inversion rules can apply unless an exception such as substantial business activities in India is met.
Is a reverse flip taxable in India?
Indian tax law has exemptions for qualifying amalgamations and for shareholders receiving shares in them, so an inbound merger can be tax-neutral in India if conditions are met; share swaps need separate capital-gains analysis.

Please note: this guide is general information based on the rules as they stood on the review date above. Regulations and RBI/CBDT procedures change often — please get advice on your specific facts before acting.
